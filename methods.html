Methods

Approach

Every indicator follows the same pipeline:

  1. Download open data with a script in R/. Raw files stay local and are not committed to Git.
  2. Check against a published figure wherever one exists, so errors in my processing would show up.
  3. Summarise into a small tidy file in data/processed/.
  4. Assess status, trend, and confidence, recorded in data/lookup/indicator_status.csv, which drives the report card.
  5. Render with Quarto.

How status, trend, and confidence are assigned

These are my independent judgements, written down so they are transparent and repeatable. They are not official assessments.

Indicator Status rule Trend rule Confidence
Seabirds Clear concern if the main species decline by more than 20% at surveyed SPAs Direction of change, baseline to 2023 Medium: before-after design, variable baseline years, partial coverage
Seals Clear concern if the national harbour seal count is at or near its lowest and most units are declining Change between the last two national censuses High: systematic national census, consistent method
Salmon Clear concern if most stocks are in poor status Change in the number of poor stocks, 2021 to 2026 Medium: official gradings, but a short series of overlapping five-year assessments
Sea temperature Context indicator (no good or bad direction) Linear trend in annual anomalies with 95% CI; “no clear change” if the CI includes zero Medium: two sites, weekly sampling, record ends 2020
MPA coverage Generally positive if more than 30% of Scottish seas is designated Change in designated area over time (published figures) Medium: high for area, low for effectiveness of management

An epidemiologist’s lens

Several indicators borrow ideas from population health surveillance:

  • Before-after comparisons without a control group (seabirds): describe the size of change, and be explicit that this alone cannot establish cause.
  • Secular trends (seabirds, Gannet): a population that was growing before an outbreak makes a simple before-after comparison understate the impact.
  • Coverage as a response rate (seabirds): the share of the population surveyed affects how far a result generalises.
  • Counts are not populations (seals): haul-out counts are a consistent index, not a census of every animal.
  • Uncertainty reported alongside estimates (all): including when it is a minimum, as with Poisson intervals on large counts.
