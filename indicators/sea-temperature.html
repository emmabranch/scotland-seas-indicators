Sea temperature

Long-term change at Stonehaven and Loch Ewe, Scotland’s two coastal ecosystem monitoring sites

What this measures

Near-surface sea temperature, sampled weekly by boat at the Scottish Coastal Observatory’s two long-term ecosystem monitoring sites:

  • Stonehaven, about 3 km off the Aberdeenshire coast in the North Sea, sampled since 1997.
  • Loch Ewe, a sea loch in Wester Ross on the Atlantic-influenced west coast, sampled since 1999.

I express each month as an anomaly: how much warmer or cooler it was than the 1999 to 2020 average for that calendar month. Near-surface samples at Loch Ewe begin in mid-2002, so its usable series and baseline effectively start in 2003. This removes the seasonal cycle so that years can be compared fairly.

Why it matters

Temperature shapes almost everything else in a marine ecosystem: plankton timing, fish distribution, seabird food supply, and the spread of warm-water species. This is a context indicator. It has no “good” or “bad” direction on its own, but it helps explain change in the biological indicators on this site.

Over roughly two decades, year-to-year variability is large compared with the long-term trend, so a single unusually warm or cold year can move the fitted line. That is why I report the confidence interval and not just the slope.

Why no clear trend?

This does not mean Scottish seas are not warming. It is a question of time window:

  • Over the long term, Scottish waters (coastal and oceanic) have warmed by about 0.05 to 0.07°C per decade over 1870 to 2016, with faster warming over the most recent 30 years of that period (Scotland’s Marine Assessment 2020).
  • Over the shorter, more recent window these two sites cover, Scotland’s Marine Assessment 2020 also reports no significant linear trend in the most recent decade. It links a cold anomaly in the waters west of Scotland to strong ocean heat loss from the winter of 2013 to 2014.
  • Two sites over about 20 years cannot separate a long-term trend from multi-decadal natural variability in the North Atlantic.

So the honest reading is: no detectable trend at these two sites over 1997 to 2020, consistent with the published regional picture, and not evidence against long-term warming. Adding the 2021 onwards data, once published, would be the most useful next step.

Quality control

I followed the data owner’s flag guidance:

  • Rejected: QF 3 (probably bad), QF 4 (bad), and QF 9 (missing).
  • Kept: QF 1, 2, 5, and 8 (good, probably good, changed, or interpolated).
  • Kept but counted: QF 0 (not yet quality controlled) and QF 10 (phenomenon uncertain), so their share is visible.
  • Extra screen: values outside 0 to 22°C are removed as implausible for these sites.

Confidence

Medium. These are carefully quality-controlled observations from fixed sites with a consistent method. But:

  • They are two points, not a picture of all Scottish seas.
  • Weekly sampling can miss short events such as marine heatwaves.
  • The published record currently ends in 2020, so the most recent years are not included.

Limitations

  • The record is about 20 years long. That is short for separating a long-term trend from natural variability such as the North Atlantic Oscillation.
  • Months with missing visits make monthly means less certain. Years with fewer than 9 months of data are excluded from the annual series, which drops 2020 at Stonehaven.
  • Near-surface means the shallowest sample on each visit, up to 5 m. Bottom water can behave differently, especially at Stonehaven, where the water column stratifies in summer.
  • Loch Ewe is a sheltered sea loch, so it is not fully representative of open west-coast waters.
