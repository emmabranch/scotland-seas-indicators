Seabirds after avian flu

Featured deep dive: what Scottish colony counts tell us before and after the 2021-22 HPAI outbreak, read through an epidemiologist’s lens

Status
✕ Clear concern
Trend
↘ Declining
Confidence
●●○ Medium
Period
2014-2023

The short version

  • At the nine Scottish SPAs surveyed for Great Skua, breeding territories fell from 6,570 to 1,524 (-77%).
  • At six Scottish Northern Gannet SPAs, nest counts fell from 169,054 to 131,361 (-22%). Against a baseline projected forward for pre-outbreak growth, the fall is -28%.
  • Not every species declined everywhere. Guillemot counts, for example, rose at some SPAs and fell at others.
  • These are before-and-after counts at surveyed sites. They show a large drop that coincides with the outbreak, but on their own they cannot prove how much of it HPAI caused.

What this measures

The change in the number of breeding seabirds at Scottish Special Protection Areas (SPAs) between a pre-outbreak baseline (mostly from the Seabirds Count census, 2015 to 2021) and a coordinated survey in 2023, after highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI H5N1) caused mass seabird deaths in 2021 and 2022. Counts are of breeding units: apparently occupied nests, sites, or territories, or individual birds for Guillemot.

Why it matters

Scotland holds internationally important seabird populations, many protected by SPAs under the Habitats Regulations. HPAI arrived on top of existing declines: the Seabirds Count census found that most seabird species in Scotland had already declined since the previous census. Seabird abundance and breeding success are OSPAR and UK Marine Strategy indicators of marine bird status.

Reading this like an outbreak investigation

What the data can tell us

  • Magnitude: how much smaller breeding numbers were in 2023 at the sites surveyed.
  • Distribution: which species and which colonies changed most.
  • Timing: the drop coincides with the outbreak, and for some species it is far outside their recent trend.

What it cannot tell us on its own

  • Cause: there is no unexposed comparison group, so this is a before-after design. Other pressures were operating at the same time.
  • Mechanism: fewer breeding birds could mean deaths, or survivors skipping a breeding season.
  • The full picture: only surveyed sites are included, and mortality after early 2023 is not captured.

The epidemiological questions behind each chart:

  1. Is the change real or noise? The forest plot shows each colony’s change with a confidence interval.
  2. What would have happened anyway? For Gannet, the report projects each colony’s pre-outbreak trend forward to 2021. Comparing against that projection is like adjusting for a secular trend.
  3. Who was counted? Coverage (the share of the population surveyed) works like a response rate, and it varies a lot between species.

Trend and status

UK picture: 13 species

Colony by colony: Great Skua and Gannet

The confidence intervals are narrow because these are large counts. That makes them a minimum estimate of uncertainty: they reflect counting noise only, not observer error, survey timing, or differences between baseline years. Even so, the Great Skua declines are far too large and too consistent to be explained by noise.

Gannet: how much depends on the baseline?

Gannet colonies were growing before the outbreak, and some baselines date from 2014. Comparing 2023 with the counted baseline gives -22%. Comparing it with each colony’s projected 2021 size gives -28%. This is the same logic as adjusting for a secular trend: if a population was rising, a simple before-after comparison understates the impact.

Confidence

Medium. The 2023 survey was large and coordinated, and for Great Skua, Gannet, and the terns it covered most of the population. But:

  • Baseline years vary (2014 to 2021), so some of each change happened before the outbreak.
  • A few 2023 counts are flagged by the report authors as preliminary or low confidence.
  • Coverage is low for some species, so totals may not represent the whole population.

Limitations

  • Attribution: the report’s authors consider HPAI the main driver for Great Skua, Gannet, and Roseate Tern, and likely for Sandwich and Common Tern. For species already in long-term decline, separating HPAI from other pressures is harder. This page reports change; it does not estimate a causal effect.
  • Breeding numbers only: the data do not include breeding success or adult survival.
  • Incomplete timing: further HPAI deaths in 2023 happened after these counts.
  • Transcription: SPA figures were transcribed from the report’s PDF tables (table and page recorded for each row) and checked against printed totals where available.

Optional next step

With approved access to the Seabird Monitoring Programme database (BTO/JNCC), annual counts at monitored colonies would show whether populations have started to recover since 2023, and allow a proper interrupted time-series analysis. Any such analysis would follow the SMP data use policy.
